Part 7 — Free Agency & Exceptions: The Tools for Teams Without Cap Space

(New segment. Dollar figures use your own league's CBA numbers for the 2026-27 season — cap $165,000,000 / tax $201,000,000 / first apron $209,000,000 / second apron $222,000,000 — since they're realistic and already wired into your web app. Real NBA figures for any given season will be close to, but not identical to, these. Glossary at the end.)

Segments 3 and 4 answered the question of how a team re-signs its own free agents. This segment answers the other half: what can a team, over the cap, actually offer everyone else?

The answer is a small toolbox of league-wide exceptions — available to every team, not tied to any specific player's history with you — each with its own size, length, and apron-based eligibility rules. Learning this toolbox is what lets you read a headline like "the Lakers used their taxpayer MLE" and immediately know their cap situation, their contract length limit, and roughly how big a piece they were able to add.

There's a fourth tool that isn't really a "free agency" exception at all but belongs in the same conversation because it lets you add outside salary without cap space: the Trade Exception, covered at the end of this segment.

The Three Versions of the Mid-Level Exception

The MLE isn't one tool — it's three, and which one you get depends entirely on your team's cap position on the day you use it.

Three important rules govern how these interact:

1. Using the Non-Taxpayer or the Bi-Annual Exception hard-caps you at the first apron for the rest of the season. This is the single most important practical consequence in this whole segment — it's why a team hovering near the first apron has to think hard before using its full MLE, since doing so removes the option of ever going over the first apron later that year, even via a trade.

2. Using the Taxpayer MLE hard-caps you at the second apron. Same logic, one tier higher — you can still go into the tax and past the first apron, but the second apron becomes an absolute ceiling for the rest of the season.

3. You cannot use the Room MLE and the Taxpayer MLE in the same season — you get one or the other, not both — and the Bi-Annual Exception is also unavailable to any team that has already used the Room or Taxpayer MLE that season. The BAE only pairs with the full Non-Taxpayer MLE.

Addition — an advanced wrinkle worth knowing about, not memorizing: a team hovering right around the first apron can sign one or more players using the Non-Taxpayer MLE for one- or two-year deals, and — as long as the total first-year salary doesn't exceed the Taxpayer MLE amount — retroactively have that signing treated as if it used the Taxpayer MLE instead. That lets the team keep the door open to exceed the first apron later in the season, at the cost of giving up further access to the full MLE. It's a niche move, but it explains why you'll occasionally see a team sign a modest one-year deal with what looks like "full MLE" money and still operate above the first apron a month later.

The "bi-annual" name refers to the once-every-two-years limit, not the contract length. As Segment 1's memory notes already established, the BAE and the MLE can each fund a different player in the same offseason, but never the same player — you can't stack them together on one contract.

The Minimum Salary Exception

This is the one tool every team has, regardless of cap position, tax status, or apron status — even a team already over the second apron can always sign a player to a minimum-salary contract.

Property Value
2026-27 minimum (1 year of experience) $2,185,633
2026-27 minimum (2+ years of experience, reimbursement rate) $2,450,000
Max years 2

The reimbursement rule is the part that surprises people. A 12-year veteran signing a minimum contract is legally entitled to the full veteran-minimum salary for someone with his experience — which is meaningfully higher than a rookie's minimum. But the league reimburses the signing team for the difference between the two-year-veteran rate and what that player actually earns, so the team's cap hit is capped at the two-year-veteran-minimum rate no matter how many years of experience the player actually has. This is exactly the mechanism your earlier notes flagged: a 10+ year veteran on a minimum deal only costs the team the two-year reimbursement rate on the cap sheet, even though he's paid his full, higher salary in real dollars.

This is why cap-strapped contenders can still fill out a bench with genuine 10-15 year veterans — the players get paid properly, but the team's books only see the smaller number.

Trade Exceptions (TPEs)

A trade exception is generated whenever a team sends out more salary than it takes back in a trade — for example, trading away a $15M player for a $5M player and a draft pick, or trading a player for nothing but picks and cash.

Team trades away:  $15M salary
Team receives back:  $5M player + picks
                          │
                          ▼
        Trade Exception created: ~$10M

        (Roughly the gap between what
        was sent out and what came back,
        plus a small standard adjustment)

That $10M exception doesn't disappear — it can be "banked" and used within one year to acquire a player (or players) from another team, up to that dollar amount, without needing to send matching salary back in return. It's essentially a one-directional cap-space voucher, usable only in trades.

Two restrictions matter most in practice:

  • A trade exception expires exactly one year after it's created. Use it or lose it.
  • Teams above the second apron cannot use trade exceptions generated in a prior season. Combined with the fact that second-apron teams also can't aggregate salaries or send cash in trades, this makes trade-based additions extremely narrow once you're that far over the line.

Key Takeaways

Free agency for an over-the-cap team without Bird Rights on a given player runs entirely through this exception toolbox: three flavors of the Mid-Level Exception depending on your exact cap position, a once-every-two-years Bi-Annual Exception, an always-available Minimum Salary Exception with a favorable reimbursement quirk for veterans, and Trade Exceptions banked from prior trades. Every one of these tools narrows as you cross the first and second aprons — and several of them, if used, actively lock you into a hard cap you cannot cross for the rest of the season. Knowing which tools survive at your specific cap position, before you ever pick up the phone with an agent, is what separates a front office that plans its offseason from one that discovers its constraints in real time.

Glossary (terms used in this segment)

  • Mid-Level Exception (MLE) — A family of three cap exceptions (Non-Taxpayer, Taxpayer, Room) letting an over-the-cap team sign outside free agents; which version you get depends on your cap position.
  • Hard cap — A rule that makes a specific apron threshold an absolute ceiling for the rest of the season, typically triggered by using the Non-Taxpayer MLE, the BAE, or acquiring a player via sign-and-trade.
  • Bi-Annual Exception (BAE) — A smaller exception usable only once every two seasons, unavailable above the first apron.
  • Minimum Salary Exception — The always-available tool for signing players to veteran-minimum contracts, with a reimbursement rule that keeps the cap hit low regardless of the player's actual experience-based salary.
  • Trade Exception (TPE) — A banked, one-directional allowance created by trading away more salary than you receive, usable within one year to acquire a player without sending matching salary back.
  • Reimbursement rate — The two-year veteran minimum salary figure used as the cap charge for any veteran-minimum signee, regardless of how many years of experience he actually has.
