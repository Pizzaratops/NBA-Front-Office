Part 5 — The GM Workflow: The Exact Order an NBA Front Office Works Through an Offseason

(New segment, written in the same style as the earlier revisions. All specific deadlines below are checked against the current, 2023 Collective Bargaining Agreement and recent reporting on how they've actually been applied. A few dates shift slightly year to year — noted where relevant. Glossary at the end.)

Everything in Segments 1 through 4 was conceptual: how a GM thinks, what team salary means, why Bird Rights exist, why cap holds force real decisions. This segment puts all of it on a calendar.

That matters because the CBA doesn't just tell you what you're allowed to do — it tells you in what order you're allowed to do it, and several of those steps are deadline-locked. A GM who understands the mechanics perfectly but loses track of the calendar can lose a player's Bird Rights, a rookie's cost-controlled extension window, or a trade opportunity simply by missing a date. This is where the CBA stops being a rulebook and becomes an operational workflow.

Addition: treat these month labels as approximate anchors, not guarantees. The league publishes an official calendar every season with exact dates (the trade deadline in particular moves depending on the schedule), so always check the current season's calendar for the precise day — but the sequence and the logic behind each step almost never change from year to year.

Step 1: The Forced Early Decisions (Late June)

Before free agency even opens, a GM has to resolve every decision the CBA puts on a clock. These are not choices you can delay — miss the deadline and the decision gets made for you, usually in the way least favorable to your flexibility.

Player and team options. Standard contract options must be exercised or declined by 5:00 PM ET on June 29. (There's a narrower carve-out: if declining a player option would make that player a restricted free agent, the option decision is due a few days earlier, by June 25, so the league has time to process qualifying offers on schedule.)

Rookie-scale team options are different — they run on their own calendar in the fall, not in June. More on that below.

Qualifying Offers (QOs). If you want to keep the right to match another team's offer for a player who would otherwise become an unrestricted free agent — turning him into a restricted free agent instead — you must tender the Qualifying Offer by the same June 29, 5:00 PM ET deadline. Miss it, and the player becomes a full unrestricted free agent; you lose all matching rights, permanently, for that summer.

This is why a real front office's June actually looks nothing like a fan's mental image of free agency starting with a bang on July 1. By the time July 1 arrives, the roster's shape — who's under contract, who's a cap hold, who's restricted, who's gone for good — has already been set weeks earlier.

Step 2: Build the Real Cap Sheet

With options and QOs resolved, the GM now has a stable roster to calculate against. This is where Segment 2's process — guaranteed salary, plus cap holds, plus dead money, plus roster charges, plus likely bonuses — gets run for real, using the new season's cap, tax, and apron numbers (the league finalizes these figures shortly before the new league year begins, since they're tied to the prior season's basketball-related income).

Only now does the GM actually know, with real numbers:

  • Are we a cap-space team, a taxpayer team, or an apron team?
  • Which exceptions do we have?
  • What is realistic to pursue?

This decision doesn't have to be finalized in June. As Segment 4 explained, the smart approach is usually to delay renouncing for as long as possible while pursuing outside targets, only giving up rights when cap room is actually needed to complete a specific deal.

Step 4: The Moratorium — Negotiate, Don't Sign

From roughly July 1 through July 5, the NBA enters a moratorium period. During this window, teams and players (both their own free agents and outside targets) can agree to contract terms "in principle" — this is when you hear reports like "Player X agrees to a four-year, $120 million deal" — but nothing can be officially signed or filed with the league office yet. Trades are also frozen during this stretch.

This period exists partly to give the league time to finalize the new season's cap, tax, and apron figures, and partly to let the frenzy of Day 1 tampering-adjacent conversations settle into an orderly signing period rather than a chaotic contract-filing race.

Step 5: The Signing Period Opens

Once the moratorium lifts, teams begin officially signing the deals that were agreed to on paper. The order in which a front office works through its list generally follows the priority structure from Segment 1 — championship equity and star retention first, financial efficiency last — but in practice it also follows a cap-driven order:

1. Re-sign your own core Bird-Rights free agents first
   (this doesn't cost cap space — you're just replacing
   a cap hold with an actual contract)

2. Use cap space (if you have any) on your top external target

3. Use remaining exceptions (MLE, BAE, minimums) to fill
   out the roster

4. Only now consider renouncing remaining cap holds, if a
   later target requires the room

Notice the logic: signing your own Bird-Rights players doesn't cost anything extra relative to their cap hold in most cases — replacing a $28M hold with a $28M contract is often close to cap-neutral, or even frees up a little room if the actual deal comes in below the hold. That's why teams typically handle their own free agents before shopping externally, not after.

If no offer sheet ever materializes, the player can still simply re-sign with his own team, or accept the one-year Qualifying Offer itself (which then makes him an unrestricted free agent the following summer). Restricted free agents who want to sign an offer sheet with a new team must do so by March 1 — after that date, they can no longer change teams that season and must either re-sign with their own team or sit out the year.

Addition: this is exactly the kind of situation your own league's commissioner deadlines (like an August 1 RFA decision date) are standing in for. Real NBA restricted free agency doesn't have a single hard deadline in early August — it can drag out for weeks because the process is driven by whenever a rival team actually produces an offer sheet, not by a fixed calendar date. A fantasy league naturally needs a firm cutoff to keep the offseason moving, so it's a reasonable simplification of a process that, in real life, has no fixed finish line until March 1.

Step 7: Rookie-Scale Options and Extensions (October)

While free agency and trades dominate the summer, a completely separate clock is running for players still on their rookie-scale contracts:

  • Third- and fourth-year team options on rookie-scale deals must be exercised or declined by October 31 of the prior season — a full year ahead of when that option season would actually begin.
  • Rookie-scale extensions (a new, longer-term deal signed before the player even reaches free agency) can be negotiated starting after his third season and must be finalized by the day before the regular season begins in his fourth season. Miss that date, and the player instead plays out his fourth season and becomes a restricted free agent the following summer.

This is precisely the "extension bomb" timeline Segment 1 warned about: a rookie's cheap Years 1–2 salary tells you almost nothing about what his Year 5 salary will look like once an extension or a max restricted-free-agency offer sheet arrives.

Step 8: Living With Trade Restrictions During the Season

Once the season starts, a GM's workflow shifts from signing to trading, but a newly signed player usually isn't tradable right away. Under the current CBA:

  • A free agent who signs a new standard contract generally cannot be traded until December 15, or three months after the date he signed — whichever is later.
  • A smaller group of players — those who re-signed with their own team using Bird or Early Bird Rights, on a team that was over the cap, with a raise larger than 120% of their prior salary — face a slightly later restriction, until January 15 instead of December 15.
  • Two-way contract players face a shorter, 30-day restriction.
  • Certain veteran extensions or renegotiations with large raises carry a longer, 6-month restriction from the date signed.
Player signs on...           Earliest trade-eligible date
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Early July                → December 15
September                 → December 15
Late September/October    → 3 months after signing date
Own-team Bird re-sign,
big raise, over the cap   → January 15
Two-way contract          → 30 days after signing
Certain extensions        → 6 months after signing

This is exactly why the trade market is quiet in October and November and suddenly wakes up around mid-December — a large share of the league's free agents become tradable on almost the same day.

Step 9: The Trade Deadline and Buyout Market

The trade deadline itself lands in early-to-mid February each season (the league sets the exact date individually every year, so always confirm the current one rather than assuming it repeats). After the deadline, teams that miss the playoffs but still want to move veterans often turn to the buyout market — negotiating with a player to waive part of his remaining guaranteed salary in exchange for release, so he can sign elsewhere as a free agent (often with a contender) for the stretch run.

Key Takeaways

The NBA offseason is not a single event; it is a sequence of deadline-locked decisions, most of which are irreversible once passed. A front office's real skill is not just knowing the rules — it's knowing exactly when each rule stops being optional. Options and Qualifying Offers lock in the roster's shape in late June, before most fans have even started paying attention. The moratorium separates agreeing to a deal from signing it. Rookie-scale decisions run on their own October calendar, completely detached from the summer's free agency frenzy. And trade eligibility unlocks in predictable waves through the season, which is why the trade market has a rhythm that closely tracks the calendar rather than pure basketball logic.

Understanding this sequence is what allows a GM to plan several moves ahead instead of reacting to each deadline as it arrives.

Glossary (terms used in this segment)

  • Moratorium — The early-July period when teams and free agents can agree to contract terms but cannot officially sign them; trades are also frozen.
  • Qualifying Offer (QO) — A one-year contract offer a team must extend by the June 29 deadline to preserve its right to match offers for an about-to-expire player, making him a restricted rather than unrestricted free agent.
  • Offer sheet — A contract a restricted free agent signs with a new team; his original team then has a short window (about two days) to match it and keep him.
  • Rookie-scale team option — The team's right to lock in a first-round pick's third and fourth contract seasons; decided each October, a full year ahead of the season it covers.
  • Rookie-scale extension — A new, longer contract negotiated after a rookie's third season and before his fourth season begins, replacing the need to wait for restricted free agency.
  • Trade eligibility date — The earliest date a newly signed player can legally be included in a trade, which varies depending on how and when he signed.
  • Buyout — A negotiated agreement where a player gives up part of his guaranteed money in exchange for being released, so he can sign elsewhere as a free agent.
