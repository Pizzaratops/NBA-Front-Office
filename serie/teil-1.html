Part 1 — The GM Mindset: How NBA Executives Actually Think

(Revised version — corrections, additions, and clarifications marked inline. A short glossary for non-native English readers is at the end.)

Forget the CBA (Collective Bargaining Agreement — the legal contract between the NBA and the players' union that sets all salary and roster rules) for a moment. The biggest mistake new fans — and many new fantasy players — make is thinking an NBA GM's job is "get the best players possible."

That is not the job.

The job is:

Win as many future games as possible Not just this season, but across the next 3–7 years, while operating under CBA constraints and ownership limits.

Maintain optionality Optionality means keeping choices open. A team with high optionality can still sign a max free agent, make a big trade, or pivot its plan next summer. A team with low optionality has already locked itself into one path, whether that path works out or not.

Avoid irreversible mistakes A bad contract can usually be traded eventually — teams absorb bad money all the time if the price (draft picks, young players) is right. But some mistakes cannot be undone: losing Bird Rights (explained in Segment 6) by renouncing them, triggering a hard cap at the wrong moment, or crossing an apron threshold that removes tools you needed later in the season. Correction from the original draft: Bird Rights are not "lost" automatically — a team loses them only by waiving-and-not-re-signing a player who then signs elsewhere, or by explicitly renouncing them. This distinction matters and will be covered fully in Segment 6.

Everything else — cap space, Bird Rights, exceptions, trades, draft picks — is just a set of tools for those three goals.

The first mental shift: NBA teams are portfolios, not lineups

A new GM looks at this:

Player Salary
Star A $52M
Starter B $28M
Starter C $22M
Young Wing $8M
Veteran Center $5M

(Note: these numbers are illustrative round figures for teaching purposes, not tied to any real season's cap. Your own league's actual 2026-27 cap figures — $165M cap / $201M tax / $209M first apron / $222M second apron — will be used starting in Segment 2.)

An experienced GM sees:

Asset category What it can become
Star A Championship engine
Starter B Trade ballast (salary "filler" that helps two sides match salaries in a trade) + rotation value
Starter C Extension decision
Young Wing Appreciating asset
Veteran Center Expiring salary / trade-deadline piece

Every contract is evaluated on future optionality, not just current performance.

The Four Questions Every GM Asks First

When you are hired, before free agency, before trades, before calling agents, you ask four questions.

Question 1: What is our competitive window?

This determines everything. A "competitive window" is the period during which a roster is realistically good enough to win a championship or compete deep into the playoffs.

Title contender — win now A roster genuinely built to win a championship this season or next. - Pay luxury tax willingly - Use every exception available (even ones that hard-cap the team, since flexibility later matters less than winning now) - Prioritize proven veterans over unproven upside - Accept reduced future flexibility if it meaningfully increases title odds

Mindset: "Future flexibility is less valuable than maximizing the next two playoff runs."

Playoff team — balance Good, but not clearly a top contender. - Avoid the second apron if possible (its restrictions — no taxpayer MLE, no aggregating salaries in trades, a frozen future first-round pick after repeated second-apron seasons — are severe and covered in Segment 3) - Keep trade flexibility - Preserve draft capital - Don't overpay role players

Mindset: "Improve without locking ourselves into mediocrity."

Rising young team — optionality A team with good young talent that is not yet ready to contend. - Preserve cap flexibility - Avoid long, expensive veteran contracts that will still be on the books when the team is ready to win - Prioritize development and draft assets - Keep room to offer future max-salary extensions to the core

Mindset: "Today's flexibility becomes tomorrow's contender."

Rebuild — asset accumulation Wins are not the primary objective; future assets are. - Take on undesirable contracts in exchange for draft picks (teams will pay you, in picks, to take a bad contract off their books) - Maximize draft position - Convert expiring veteran contracts into future assets before they leave for nothing - Avoid paying for short-term wins that only hurt draft position without building anything

Mindset: "Every roster spot is an asset-conversion opportunity."

Addition: real front offices reassess this window at least once a year, and sometimes mid-season. A "rising young team" that has a breakout year can suddenly behave like a "playoff team" by the trade deadline. Treat these four categories as a spectrum you move along, not a permanent label.

This is the most important strategic decision of the entire offseason — every later decision (Segments 2–10) is filtered through it.

Question 2: Who are our "untouchables"?

Not because they can never be traded — in the NBA, almost every player has been traded at some point — but because every other decision is built around them.

Example: - Franchise star (age 26): build around - All-Star wing (age 24): likely core piece - Recent lottery pick: development priority

Everyone else becomes: - a rotation piece, - a trade asset, - salary-matching material (a contract whose main current value is that its salary number helps a trade satisfy CBA matching rules — this is explained fully in Segment 8), - or an expiring contract (a contract in its final season, which other teams may want purely to clear future cap space).

A veteran GM usually has 3–5 true core assets and treats the rest as movable.

Question 3: What are the future extension bombs?

This is where inexperienced GMs get trapped. An "extension" is a new contract signed before a player's current one expires, usually because a player becomes eligible to negotiate one at a fixed point (rookie-scale players become extension-eligible after their third season, for example — the exact rules and raise limits are covered in Segment 8).

Suppose:

Player Current salary
Young Star $12M
Young Wing $8M
Center $18M

Today the team looks cheap.

But next summer:

Player Extension
Young Star $38M
Young Wing $28M

Suddenly payroll jumps by $46M.

Experienced GMs are always looking 2–3 summers ahead. They ask: - Who becomes extension-eligible, and when? - Who will likely command a max or near-max salary? - Can we keep everyone without entering the second apron? - If not, who is the odd man out?

This is why teams sometimes trade good young players earlier than fans expect — they are pricing in a contract that hasn't been signed yet.

Question 4: What constraints does ownership impose?

The CBA says what is legal. Ownership decides what is affordable.

Example:

Team Ownership instruction
Big-market contender "Spend whatever it takes."
Mid-market team "Tax is okay; second apron only for a real contender."
Cost-conscious owner "Stay below the tax line if possible."

A GM who ignores ownership constraints gets fired, even if the basketball logic is sound. In your NBAFO league, this is effectively set by whatever spending posture your commissioner (Daniel) and league rules assume for each franchise — but the CBA itself never forces a team to pay the tax; that is always an ownership choice layered on top of the legal minimum requirements (the salary floor, covered in Segment 2).

The Front Office Priority Pyramid

This is a hierarchy — not an official NBA rule, but a widely used way of thinking about priorities — that most executives implicitly follow, from most important (top) to least important (bottom):

  1. Championship equity — Can this move materially increase our title odds?
  2. Star retention — Keep franchise-level talent and maintain pathways to retain future stars (largely via Bird Rights).
  3. Asset preservation — Protect picks, young players, Bird Rights, and movable contracts from unnecessary loss.
  4. Financial efficiency — Stay under useful thresholds when the competitive gain is small; optimize tax and apron positioning.
  5. Short-term roster perfection — Bench upgrades, positional balance, and marginal rotation improvements come last.

Fans often optimize the bottom layer first — arguing about the 13th roster spot. GMs optimize from the top down.

A Concrete Example: Why Good Players Get Traded

Imagine you have:

Player Salary
Star Guard $48M
Star Forward $42M
Excellent 3&D Wing $27M
Young Center $15M
Role Players $35M

Total: $167M

Next year the wing wants $38M as an extension.

If you extend him, payroll rises to $178M+, pushing you deep into tax or apron territory (the exact consequence depends on where the actual thresholds sit that season — see Segment 3).

A fan says: "Keep everyone."

A GM asks: - Does the wing increase championship odds enough to justify losing future flexibility? - Will the second apron prevent us from improving the roster later, through trades or exceptions? - Can we replace 80% of his production with a $15M player plus preserved flexibility?

Sometimes the answer is yes, sometimes no. The key point is that the decision is portfolio optimization, not player ranking — you are not asking "is he good," you are asking "is he the best use of this roster spot and this money, given everything else we want to do."

The Single Most Important Habit: Always Think in Timelines

Every player is best understood as belonging to one timeline, though — as noted above — teams themselves can shift between timelines faster than expected.

Timeline Typical move
0–2 years (title window) Veteran acquisitions, tax spending, use of the MLE
2–4 years (core development) Extensions, selective trades, upside bets
4–7 years (asset accumulation) Draft picks, young prospects, cap flexibility

Bad GMs mix timelines. Example: a rebuilding team signs a 31-year-old role player to four expensive guaranteed years. By the time the team is actually good, that player is declining and his contract blocks the flexibility the team will need at that point.

Good GMs align contract length and money with the competitive window: short, value deals during a rebuild; longer, higher-priced deals only once the core is set and the window is genuinely open.

Your Mental Model Before We Touch the CBA

When you become GM, your first day is not: - "How much cap space do we have?" - "What exception can I use?"

Your first day is:

What is our window? Contend now, bridge, rise, or rebuild. This determines whether flexibility or immediate wins is more valuable.

Who is the core? Identify the 3–5 players around whom every other decision will be organized.

What future extensions are coming? Project two to three summers ahead so today's bargain contracts do not become tomorrow's apron trap.

What spending level will ownership support? Separate what the CBA allows from what ownership is willing to fund in tax and apron territory.

Only after those four answers exist do you open the cap sheet.

Glossary (terms used in this segment)

  • CBA — Collective Bargaining Agreement; the rulebook governing salaries, contracts, and roster rules, negotiated between the NBA and the players' union. The current one took effect in 2023.
  • Optionality — Having multiple realistic paths still open (trades, signings, extensions) rather than being locked into one plan.
  • Hard cap — A rule that temporarily makes the salary cap an absolute limit a team cannot cross for the rest of the season, usually triggered by using certain exceptions.
  • Apron — A salary threshold above the luxury tax line. There are two (first and second apron); crossing them removes specific team-building tools rather than just costing more money. Fully explained in Segment 3.
  • Bird Rights — A team's right to re-sign its own free agent above the cap. Explained fully in Segment 6.
  • Expiring contract — A contract in its final guaranteed season; valuable to other teams mainly because it clears cap space next summer.
  • Trade ballast / salary filler — A contract added to a trade mainly to help the salaries match under CBA rules, not for its basketball value.
  • Salary matching — The CBA requirement that, in most trades, the salaries teams send and receive must fall within certain percentage limits of each other.
  • Extension-eligible — A player who has reached the point in his contract where the CBA allows him to sign a new deal before his current one expires.
  • Portfolio (in this context) — The whole set of a team's contracts, picks, and cap tools, evaluated together rather than player-by-player.
